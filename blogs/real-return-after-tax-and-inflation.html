Your Real Return: What Is Left After Tax and Inflation

Most return figures you see are quoted before anything is taken out. The bank quotes 7%. The fund factsheet quotes 14%. The property broker quotes how much the flat has "doubled". None of these is the number that decides whether your family is actually getting richer. That number is your real rate of return: what is left after tax, after costs, and after inflation has quietly marked down the value of every rupee.

By SELEQT Editorial

Retail inflation in India rose to 5.54% in September 2026, the highest reading in nearly two years. That makes this a good moment to do the arithmetic most portfolios never get put through.

The three deductions nobody quotes

A headline return passes through three filters before it reaches you. Tax takes its share, either every year or when you sell. Costs take theirs, whether as an expense ratio, a management fee, brokerage, or the spread on a product you never saw itemised. Inflation takes the last share, and it is the one that never appears on a statement.

The order matters less than the habit of applying all three. An investor who looks only at pre-tax returns will consistently prefer the wrong products, because the products with the highest headline numbers are often the ones that lose the most to tax and fees.

The fixed deposit that loses money

Take a 7% fixed deposit held by someone in the 30% bracket. Interest is taxed every year at slab rate, so after 30% tax plus 4% cess, the 7% becomes roughly 4.8%. If the investor's income attracts surcharge, the effective rate under the new regime can reach 39%, and the post-tax yield falls to about 4.3%.

Now set that against inflation of 5% to 5.5%. The real return is negative. The deposit grows on paper every quarter, and the investor feels prudent, while the purchasing power of the money falls by a little each year. Nothing about this is visible on a passbook, which is exactly why it persists.

This does not make fixed deposits a bad instrument. They do a specific job, which we discussed in building the conservative part of your portfolio. The mistake is using them for money that has a ten-year horizon, where their job is the wrong one.

Equity: the value of tax you have not paid yet

Equity is taxed differently, and the difference is larger than the rates suggest. Long-term gains on listed equity are taxed at 12.5% above ₹1.25 lakh, and only when you sell. Until then, the tax you would owe stays invested and compounds for you.

Consider ₹1 crore invested for ten years at 12% a year. It grows to about ₹3.11 crore. Tax on the gain at 12.5% comes to roughly ₹26 lakh, leaving about ₹2.84 crore. The post-tax annual return works out to around 11%, not far from the headline. Against 5% inflation, the real return is close to 5.7% a year. Over a decade, that is the difference between wealth that grows and wealth that merely keeps its shape.

The lesson is not that equity always wins. It is that deferral is an asset in its own right. Every unnecessary switch, every churn-driven redemption, and every portfolio that books gains each year to "lock in profits" gives some of that asset back. We covered the current rates in detail in the new capital gains rules.

Costs compound too

Fees look small because they are quoted as a percentage of assets, not of returns. A 1% annual fee on a portfolio earning 10% is a tenth of your return, every year.

Over long periods the gap becomes large. ₹1 crore growing at 11% for twenty years becomes about ₹8.06 crore. At 10%, it becomes about ₹6.73 crore. A single percentage point of cost, sustained for two decades, has taken roughly ₹1.3 crore, or about a sixth of the final value. Some costs earn their keep through better outcomes, but every cost should have to prove that it does.

Your inflation is not the CPI

The official inflation figure is an average basket. A family's real cost of living rarely matches it. Private school and university fees, quality healthcare, domestic help, travel, and housing in the better parts of a large city have, for most affluent households, risen faster than the headline index over long periods.

That means two families with identical portfolios can have very different real returns. If your spending is concentrated in education and healthcare, a portfolio that only matches CPI is losing ground against the things you actually buy. For NRIs, the relevant inflation may be in dirhams, dollars, or pounds, which adds a currency layer that we explored in what a falling rupee does to your returns.

How to calculate it properly

The precise formula is simple. Take one plus the post-tax, post-cost return, divide it by one plus inflation, and subtract one. A 4.8% post-tax return with 5.5% inflation gives 1.048 divided by 1.055, minus one, which is about minus 0.7%. Subtracting one rate from the other gives nearly the same answer at these levels, and that shortcut is fine for a quick check.

The harder part is honesty about the inputs. Use your actual tax bracket including surcharge, not the slab you wish you were in. Use the full cost of the product, including fees that are buried in a spread or a commission. Use an inflation figure that reflects your family's spending, and err on the higher side for long horizons.

What changes once you think this way

Three decisions usually look different afterwards. Long-horizon money tends to move out of instruments taxed at slab rate every year and into structures where tax is deferred or lower. Turnover gets treated as a cost, because it converts unrealised gains into a tax bill. And fees get evaluated against the real return they produce, not the gross return they advertise.

None of this calls for more risk. It calls for each rupee to sit in the structure that suits its time horizon, so that the return you keep is the return you were promised.

Where this leaves you

A headline return is a starting point, not a result. Strip out tax, costs and your own inflation, and some comfortable-looking holdings turn out to be standing still. The aim is not the highest number on the factsheet. It is the highest number left in your hands, in today's money.

Frequently asked questions

What is the real rate of return?
It is the return on an investment after adjusting for inflation, and ideally after tax and costs too. It tells you how much your purchasing power has actually grown. A positive nominal return can still be a negative real return.
What is the post-tax return on a fixed deposit in India?
For someone in the 30% bracket, a 7% FD yields roughly 4.8% after tax and cess. With surcharge, it can fall to about 4.3%. Against inflation of around 5%, that is a negative real return.
How is the real return calculated?
Divide one plus your after-tax return by one plus the inflation rate, then subtract one. For a quick estimate, subtract inflation from the after-tax return. Use your true marginal tax rate and all costs for an accurate picture.
Does equity always give a better real return than debt?
Not always, and not over short periods. Over long horizons, listed equity has historically delivered higher real returns, helped by lower tax rates and tax paid only on sale. Debt still has a role for stability and near-term goals.
Which inflation figure should I use?
CPI is a reasonable floor, but most affluent families face higher inflation in education, healthcare and housing. For long-term planning, using a figure one to two points above CPI is the more prudent assumption.
