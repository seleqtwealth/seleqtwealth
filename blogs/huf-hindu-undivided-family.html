The Hindu Undivided Family: Still a Useful Structure, or a Relic?

For generations, the Hindu Undivided Family was the default financial structure of Indian business families, particularly in Gujarat, Rajasthan and Maharashtra. Many families still have one, often inherited rather than chosen, and many advisers still recommend creating one. The tax benefits of an HUF are real but narrower than they once were. Its long-term consequences are more significant than most families realise when they open the PAN.

By SELEQT Editorial

This article looks at what an HUF actually does in 2026, where it still helps, and where it creates problems that are hard to undo.

What an HUF is

A Hindu Undivided Family is a family unit recognised under Hindu law, which also applies to Jain, Sikh and Buddhist families. It consists of a common ancestor and all lineal descendants, along with their spouses. The senior member who manages its affairs is the karta. Members who have a birthright in the family's property are coparceners.

For tax purposes, an HUF is a separate taxpayer. It has its own PAN, files its own return, and has its own basic exemption limit. The Income-tax Act, 2025 continues to treat the HUF as a distinct person, in the same way as its predecessor.

An HUF is not created by a deed in the way a company or trust is. It arises by operation of law, and in tax practice it is generally taken to begin when a Hindu man marries. What families do when they "create" an HUF is give it assets, a PAN and a bank account, so that it begins to hold property and earn income.

Where the tax benefit comes from

The benefit is simple: a second taxable entity with its own slabs. Income earned by the HUF is taxed in the HUF's hands, not in the karta's. If the HUF holds investments or property that produce income, that income starts in the lower slabs rather than being added on top of an individual's already high income.

Under the old tax regime, the HUF could also claim its own deductions, such as for insurance premiums or certain investments. Under the new regime, which most taxpayers now use, these deductions largely fall away, and the benefit reduces mainly to the separate slab structure.

The clubbing trap

The obstacle is how the HUF acquires its assets. If a member transfers his or her own property or money into the HUF, the income from that property is clubbed back into the member's own income. The tax law treats the transfer as if it never happened. This rule, long established under the old Act, is carried forward in the new one.

So the obvious strategy, where the karta moves part of his savings into the HUF to split the income, does not work. The income returns to his return.

What does work is income from assets that the HUF genuinely owns: ancestral property, the HUF's share on the partition of a larger family, or property left to the HUF under someone's will. Gifts are less helpful than they appear. For an HUF, only its own members count as relatives. A gift from a member is not taxed as the HUF's income, but the income it earns is clubbed back to the member who gave it. A gift from anyone else, including close family outside the HUF, is taxable in the HUF's hands once gifts from non-members exceed ₹50,000 in a year.

In practice, the families that benefit most from an HUF are those that already hold ancestral assets or expect to inherit into the HUF. For a family starting from scratch, the benefit is often much smaller than expected.

Daughters are coparceners

Since the 2005 amendment to the Hindu Succession Act, and as confirmed by the Supreme Court in 2020, daughters are coparceners by birth, with the same rights as sons, including daughters born before the amendment and those who have since married. A married daughter remains a coparcener in her father's HUF, even as she becomes a member of her husband's.

This is right and fair. It also means that the HUF's property is shared among more people than older family arrangements assumed, and that any plan to keep HUF assets within one line of the family is likely to be challenged.

The hard part: getting out

An HUF is easy to bring into use and difficult to dissolve. Ending it requires a total partition, in which all the HUF's assets are divided among the coparceners according to their shares. Each coparcener, including each daughter, is entitled to a share. Tax law does not recognise a partial partition, so the family cannot simply distribute some assets and leave the rest.

A partition can also become the moment when long-dormant disagreements surface. One member wants the house, another wants cash, a third disputes the valuation. Because every coparcener has a birthright, a single dissenting member can make the process slow and expensive.

This is why some families who are now planning succession seriously are choosing to wind down an existing HUF in an orderly way, and to hold family assets in structures such as a private trust, which can define who benefits and how, as we explored in private family trusts in India.

When an HUF still makes sense

It can still work well when the family already holds ancestral property generating significant income, when there is a reliable flow of gifts from non-member relatives, or when the family wants a simple, low-cost vehicle for shared assets and is comfortable with the rules of coparcenary ownership. It is far less useful as a fresh tax-saving device for a salaried professional or a first-generation entrepreneur.

Where this leaves you

An HUF is a tax entity built on top of a family law concept, and the family law consequences last much longer than the tax savings. Before using one, or adding more assets to an existing one, ask not only what it saves this year but how it will be divided in thirty years, and among whom.

Frequently asked questions

What are the tax benefits of an HUF?
An HUF is a separate taxpayer with its own basic exemption and slab rates, so income it earns is taxed separately from its members. Under the new regime, most deductions no longer apply, so the benefit is mainly the separate slabs.
Can I transfer my own money to my HUF to save tax?
Generally no. Income from property that a member transfers to the HUF is clubbed back into the member's own income. The benefit applies to assets the HUF acquires independently, such as ancestral property or an inheritance left to the HUF.
Are daughters coparceners in an HUF?
Yes. Since 2005, daughters are coparceners by birth with the same rights as sons. This applies whether or not they are married.
How is an HUF dissolved?
Through a total partition, in which all assets are divided among coparceners. Tax law does not recognise partial partitions. Partition can be complex if members disagree.
Is an HUF better than a family trust?
They serve different purposes. An HUF is a tax entity governed by Hindu law and fixed rules of shares. A trust allows the family to define beneficiaries and terms, and is usually more flexible for succession planning.
