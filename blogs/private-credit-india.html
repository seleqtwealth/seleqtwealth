Private Credit in India: What the 14% Yield Is Really Paying You For

Over the past few years, private credit in India has moved from a niche strategy to a staple of HNI portfolios. The pitch is simple. Banks have pulled back from lending to mid-sized companies, those companies still need capital, and investors who provide it can earn yields well above a fixed deposit or a bond fund. The pitch is broadly true. What it leaves out is why the yield is high, and what has to go right for investors to actually receive it.

By SELEQT Editorial

A higher yield is always compensation for something. In higher yield is not higher return, we looked at this in listed bonds. Private credit raises the same question with less liquidity and less transparency.

What private credit is

Private credit refers to loans made directly to companies by non-bank lenders, usually through a fund, rather than through a bank or the public bond market. In India, most private credit funds are structured as Category II Alternative Investment Funds, regulated by SEBI, with a minimum investment of ₹1 crore.

The borrowers are typically mid-sized companies that are too small or too complex for the bond market, need capital faster than a bank will provide it, or want terms a bank will not offer. Real estate developers, growth-stage companies and promoters raising capital against their holdings are common borrowers.

Not one strategy, several

"Private credit" covers a wide range of risk, and the label tells you very little until you know which kind you are buying.

Performing credit lends to healthy companies with steady cash flows, usually secured by assets or receivables. Returns are typically in the low teens, and the main risk is a borrower that weakens over the loan's life.

Real estate credit lends to developers against specific projects, with security over the land and the cash flows from sales. Returns can be higher, and so is the dependence on project execution and the property cycle.

Promoter financing lends against promoters' shareholdings, often listed shares. The security can be liquid, but its value can fall precisely when it is needed.

Special situations and distressed credit lends to or buys the debt of companies under stress. Returns can be high, but outcomes depend heavily on legal processes and the manager's ability to restructure or recover.

A fund targeting 12% and one targeting 18% are not doing the same thing better or worse. They are taking different risks.

Where the yield comes from

The yield on a private loan reflects several things at once: compensation for the borrower's credit risk, for the illiquidity of a loan that cannot be sold easily, for the complexity of structuring and monitoring it, and for the manager's fees. What matters to the investor is the return after defaults, recoveries, fees and tax.

A fund that lends at 16% and loses 4% of capital to defaults over its life will deliver a far lower return than its headline. The quality of a private credit manager shows not in the loans it makes but in what it recovers when a loan goes wrong.

The risks to weigh

Liquidity. Category II AIFs are closed-ended, typically with terms of three to six years. Your capital is committed for that period, and there is usually no reliable way to exit early.

Concentration. A fund may hold fifteen to twenty-five loans. One or two failures can materially reduce returns.

Valuation. Private loans are not traded, so the fund's reported value relies on the manager's assessment. Problems may not appear in the valuation until late.

Recovery. When a borrower defaults, recovery depends on the security, the loan documents, and the speed of legal processes, which in India can be slow.

How it is taxed

Category II AIFs are generally pass-through for tax. Income is taxed in the hands of investors as though they had earned it directly, not at the fund level. For a private credit fund, most income is interest, which is taxed at your slab rate. For an investor in the top bracket, a 14% gross yield can become roughly 8.5% to 9.6% after tax, depending on surcharge, before any defaults.

That makes private credit more attractive for investors in lower tax brackets, for entities with different tax profiles, and for NRIs investing through GIFT City funds, where the tax treatment for non-residents can be more favourable. We covered that route in investing in India through GIFT City.

Questions to ask a manager

Ask about the full history of every fund the manager has run: how many loans defaulted, how much was recovered, and how long it took. Ask what security sits behind each loan and how it is valued. Ask about the largest single exposure and the largest sector exposure. Ask how the manager is paid, and whether carry is earned only after investors receive their capital and a hurdle return. Finally, ask what the manager does when a borrower misses a payment, and for a real example.

Where it fits

For most families, private credit belongs in the alternatives sleeve of the portfolio, at a size that would not cause hardship if the capital were locked up for five years or partly lost. It can add steady, equity-independent income to a portfolio that already has a sound core of listed equity and high-quality debt. It is not a replacement for that core, and it is not a better fixed deposit.

Where this leaves you

Private credit can earn its place, but only for investors who understand what the yield is compensating them for. The question is never whether 14% is attractive. It is whether the manager will still be returning your capital when the cycle turns.

Frequently asked questions

What is private credit in India?
It is lending to companies by non-bank investors, usually through Category II AIFs. Borrowers are typically mid-sized companies, real estate developers or promoters who need capital outside the banking system.
What is the minimum investment in a private credit AIF?
₹1 crore for most investors, as prescribed by SEBI for AIFs. GIFT City funds may have different minimums in US dollars.
How are private credit AIFs taxed?
Category II AIFs are generally pass-through, so interest income is taxed in the investor's hands at slab rate. Investors in the highest bracket keep significantly less of the headline yield.
Is private credit safe?
It carries real credit, liquidity and recovery risk. Returns depend heavily on the manager's underwriting and its ability to recover money when loans go wrong.
How long is money locked in a private credit fund?
Most Category II funds are closed-ended, with terms of three to six years. Early exit is usually not possible.
