The Family Constitution: Why Business Families Fall Apart, and the Document That Helps Them Hold

Family businesses rarely fail because of a bad product or a bad market. They fail because of questions nobody asked while the founder was alive. Can a son-in-law join the business? Does the sibling who runs the company earn more than the one who does not? What happens when a cousin wants to sell? A family constitution is where those questions are asked, and answered, before they become disputes.

By SELEQT Editorial

India has more than its share of business families whose second or third generation ended up in court or in the newspapers. In nearly every case, the conflict was not about the business itself. It was about rules that everyone assumed and nobody had written down.

What a family constitution is

A family constitution, sometimes called a family charter, is a written agreement among family members about how the family will relate to its business and its shared wealth. It records the family's values, sets out who can own, who can work and who can decide, and establishes how disagreements will be resolved.

It is primarily a moral and governance document rather than a legal one. Its strength comes from the fact that every adult member has participated in writing it and has signed it. Where certain provisions need to be enforceable, they are mirrored in legal documents, which we discuss below.

The three circles

Advisers who work with family businesses often describe three overlapping groups: the family, the owners, and the managers. A founder is usually all three at once. By the second generation, the circles begin to separate. One sibling runs the company, another owns shares but lives abroad, a third has married into the family and has views but no formal role.

Most conflicts arise when someone in one circle makes a claim that belongs to another. An owner who does not work in the business expects to be consulted on hiring. A manager who is also family expects to be paid as an owner. A family constitution draws these lines clearly, so that each person knows which hat they are wearing in which conversation.

What it typically covers

Values and purpose. Why the family wants to stay in business together, and what it expects the business to stand for. This section is often dismissed as soft, but it is the one people return to in a crisis.

Employment of family members. The qualifications and outside experience required before joining, how family employees are paid, how they are evaluated, and who has the authority to let them go. Many families require several years of work elsewhere first, and pay family members at market rates for the role rather than by surname.

Ownership. Who can hold shares, whether spouses can, what happens on divorce, and whether shares can be sold or pledged. Most constitutions give the family a right of first refusal before shares can pass outside it.

Exit and liquidity. How a member who wants to leave can sell, at what valuation, and over what period. A fair exit route is one of the strongest protections against conflict, because a member who feels trapped is far more likely to litigate.

Dividends and distributions. How much of the profit is reinvested and how much is paid out, so that members who work in the business and those who do not are not constantly at odds.

Governance bodies. A family assembly of all members, which meets once a year, and a smaller family council that represents the family between meetings and liaises with the board.

Dispute resolution. A defined path, usually conversation, then mediation by a trusted outsider, then arbitration, so that disagreements do not go directly to court.

The next generation. How younger members will be educated about the business and the family's wealth, and at what age they will be given information and responsibility.

How it connects to legal documents

A family constitution sets intention. Legal documents give it teeth. The provisions on share transfers, rights of first refusal and exit valuations should be reflected in the company's articles of association and in a shareholders' agreement. Arrangements for holding shares across generations can sit in a family trust, which we explored in private family trusts in India. Individual intentions belong in each member's will.

Without that alignment, a constitution can contradict the legal position, and in a dispute the legal documents will usually prevail. The work is to make sure they say the same thing.

How one is actually written

The process matters as much as the document. It usually begins with confidential conversations with each adult family member, often conducted by an external facilitator so that people speak openly. Those conversations surface the issues that genuinely worry the family, which are rarely the ones the founder expects.

A draft is then discussed in a series of family meetings, revised, and finally signed. The whole process typically takes several months. Families that try to compress it into a single weekend usually produce a document that nobody feels bound by.

A constitution should be revisited every few years, and whenever a new generation reaches adulthood, so that those who will live under it have a hand in shaping it.

When it is needed

The ideal time is while the founder is active and respected, and before the second generation is fully in the business. At that stage, agreement is easier and the founder's authority can help settle questions that would be contentious later. Families that wait until a death or a dispute find themselves writing rules in the middle of the conflict those rules were meant to prevent.

Where this leaves you

Wealth transfers easily. Agreement does not. A family constitution is the family's attempt to pass on not just its assets but its understanding of how to share them. It cannot guarantee harmony, but it gives the next generation a framework, a process and a shared starting point, which is more than most inherit.

Frequently asked questions

What is a family constitution?
It is a written agreement among family members covering values, ownership, employment of family members, governance and dispute resolution for a family business or shared wealth. It is primarily a governance document rather than a legal contract.
Is a family constitution legally binding in India?
Generally not by itself. Key provisions on shares, transfers and exits should be mirrored in the articles of association, a shareholders' agreement or a trust deed to make them enforceable.
What is a family council?
A small group of family representatives that acts on behalf of the wider family between annual meetings. It oversees the constitution and serves as the link between the family and the company's board.
When should a family write a constitution?
Ideally while the founder is active, and before the second generation takes over fully. Writing one during a dispute is far harder.
How long does it take to create a family constitution?
Usually several months, including individual conversations, family meetings and revisions. The process of agreeing is as valuable as the final document.
