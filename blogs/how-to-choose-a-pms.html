Choosing a PMS: How to Read Past the Returns Table

Every portfolio management service is sold with a returns table. The numbers are usually impressive, the period is usually well chosen, and the comparison is usually flattering. Yet the table is the least useful part of the pitch. Knowing how to choose a PMS means knowing what the table leaves out: the fees, the tax, the benchmark, the concentration, and whether the process that produced those numbers can repeat them.

By SELEQT Editorial

A PMS requires a minimum of ₹50 lakh, and most investors commit for several years. That is a decision worth making on more than one slide.

What you are actually buying

A PMS is not a fund. The manager buys and sells securities directly in a demat account held in your name. You own every share, see every trade, and can, in principle, ask for a customised portfolio. That direct ownership is the core of the proposition and also the source of its main disadvantages, which are tax and cost.

Start with the right comparison

SEBI requires portfolio managers to report returns on a time-weighted basis and to show them against a benchmark prescribed by the Association of Portfolio Managers in India (APMI) for their strategy category. APMI publishes this data for every strategy each month.

That makes it possible to compare like with like. Before you meet a manager, look up their strategy on the APMI website and see how it has performed against its benchmark and against peers over three and five years, not just over the period chosen for the presentation. Ask, too, whether the strategy has ever changed its benchmark, and why. A recent change is worth questioning.

Fees, in full

PMS fees typically take one of three forms. A fixed fee is a percentage of assets each year, commonly in the range of 1.5% to 2.5%. A performance fee is a share of returns above a hurdle rate, often 10% to 20% of the excess. A hybrid model combines a lower fixed fee with a performance share.

Ask whether the performance fee is charged against a high-water mark, which ensures you do not pay twice for the same gains after a fall and recovery. Ask what the hurdle rate is and whether it is compounded. Ask about exit loads, which SEBI permits only during the first three years and caps on a declining scale. And add brokerage, custody and fund accounting charges to arrive at the true annual cost.

SEBI also requires managers to offer a direct onboarding option, without a distributor in between. If you are being introduced through an intermediary, it is fair to ask how that changes your costs.

The tax drag nobody shows you

Because the securities sit in your demat account, every profitable sale is taxed in your hands in the year it happens. A manager who turns over half the portfolio each year will generate short-term gains taxed at 20% and long-term gains at 12.5%, year after year, whether or not you withdraw anything.

Reported PMS returns are pre-tax. For a high-turnover strategy, the return you keep may be one to two percentage points lower each year than the return in the table. Ask for the strategy's average annual turnover and, if possible, for a sample client's realised gains over the last three years. We showed how much deferral is worth in your real return after tax and inflation.

For investors who do not need customisation, a mutual fund or a specialised investment fund running a similar strategy defers tax until redemption, which can make it the better choice even with a slightly lower gross return.

Concentration and dispersion

Many PMS strategies hold fifteen to twenty-five stocks. Concentration is how skilled managers beat the market, and also how they fall far behind it. Look at the largest positions, the sector weights, and how much of the past return came from a handful of names.

Ask about dispersion, meaning how different the returns of individual clients in the same strategy have been. Because each client's portfolio is built at a different time, two investors in the same PMS can have very different outcomes. A manager with wide dispersion either has a fragile process or a timing problem.

The process, and the people

Returns are an outcome. What you are really underwriting is the process that produced them and the people who run it. Ask how ideas are generated and how positions are sized. Ask when the manager sells, and for an example of a mistake and how it was handled. Ask who makes final decisions and what happens if that person leaves.

A good manager will answer these questions plainly and will be able to explain periods of underperformance without blaming the market. A manager who can talk only about winners is telling you something.

Fit with the rest of the portfolio

Finally, a PMS should earn its place relative to what you already own. If your mutual funds are already heavy in mid-caps, a mid-cap PMS adds concentration, not diversification. If a large part of your wealth is in a family business, a PMS focused on the same sector doubles that exposure. The right PMS is the one that fills a gap in the portfolio, not the one with the best recent year.

Where this leaves you

The returns table answers the question of what happened. Choosing well requires answering what it cost, what it will cost in tax, how it was achieved, and whether it can happen again. Those answers take an afternoon of reading. The decision they inform will run for years.

Frequently asked questions

How do I compare PMS performance?
Use the monthly data published by APMI, which shows each strategy's time-weighted returns against its prescribed benchmark. Compare over three and five years, and against peers in the same category.
What fees does a PMS charge?
Typically a fixed fee of about 1.5% to 2.5% a year, a performance fee above a hurdle rate, or a combination. Add brokerage and custody charges, and check for a high-water mark and exit loads.
Is PMS better than mutual funds?
Not inherently. A PMS offers concentration and customisation but is taxed on every trade in your hands. A mutual fund defers tax until redemption and costs less. The answer depends on your needs and the specific strategies.
What is the minimum investment for a PMS?
₹50 lakh, as prescribed by SEBI. It can be invested in cash, existing securities or a combination.
Are PMS returns shown before or after tax?
Reported returns are before tax. Because gains are taxed each year in the client's hands, the post-tax return can be noticeably lower for high-turnover strategies.
