Key Person Insurance: Protecting the Business From the Loss of One Person

In many Indian family businesses, one person holds the relationships, the technical knowledge and the confidence of the bank. The business may have a hundred employees, but its value rests on a single individual. Key person insurance, often called keyman insurance, exists for the moment that individual is no longer there. It is one of the most useful policies a promoter-led business can hold, and one of the least discussed.

By SELEQT Editorial

Personal life cover protects a family. Key person cover protects the business that the family depends on. The two are often confused, and the gap between them is where many businesses are left exposed.

What it is

Key person insurance is a life insurance policy that a company takes out on the life of an individual whose death would cause it financial loss. The company proposes the policy, pays the premium and is the beneficiary. If the key person dies during the term, the payout goes to the business, not to the individual's family.

The insured can be a promoter, a managing director, a partner, a senior technical lead, or the person who holds the largest client relationships. The test is economic, not hierarchical. Whose absence would cost the business the most?

What the money is for

The payout gives the business time and money at the moment it has least of both. It can repay a loan that the bank may call in once the promoter has gone, keep salaries and suppliers paid while revenue dips, fund the search for a replacement, and reassure lenders and customers that the business is stable.

For businesses with personal guarantees from the promoter, the cover also protects the family indirectly. Without it, a bank may turn to the guarantor's estate, putting the family home or investments at risk to settle a business debt.

How much cover makes sense

There is no single formula, but insurers and advisers generally use three approaches, often in combination.

The first is contribution to profit: a multiple of the profits attributable to the key person, reflecting how long it would take to replace them and how much would be lost in the meantime. The second is replacement cost: what it would cost to recruit, relocate and train someone of similar capability, plus the revenue lost during the transition. The third is debt cover: the outstanding loans that are secured by the key person's guarantee or that a lender is likely to recall.

Insurers will underwrite only up to an amount they consider justified by the company's financials, so the cover requested should be supported by audited accounts and a clear rationale.

The tax treatment

Under long-standing practice, premiums paid by a business on a key person policy have generally been allowed as a business expense, provided the policy is genuinely for business purposes. The payout received by the business is, correspondingly, treated as taxable business income. Unlike an individual life policy, a keyman policy does not get the exemption for maturity or death proceeds.

The Income-tax Act, 2025 has renumbered the relevant provisions, so the specific sections referenced in older guidance have changed. The underlying treatment should be confirmed with your chartered accountant for the current year.

Complications arise when a policy is assigned to the key person, for example on retirement. Tax tribunals have repeatedly looked at whether the surrender value or premiums paid at that point should be taxed in the individual's hands. The assignment should be planned and documented rather than done informally.

The cover most promoters miss: buy-sell insurance

Key person insurance protects the business. It does not answer a second, often more dangerous question: what happens to the shares of a partner who dies?

In a business with two or three promoters, the shares of a deceased partner usually pass to their heirs. The surviving partners may then find themselves in business with a spouse or child who has no involvement, different priorities, or a need for cash. The heirs, in turn, may own a stake they cannot sell.

A buy-sell arrangement solves this. The partners agree in advance, through a shareholders' agreement, that the survivors will buy the deceased partner's stake at a defined valuation. Life insurance on each partner funds the purchase. The family receives fair value in cash, and the business stays with those who run it. What to get right

Choose term insurance rather than investment-linked products, for the same reasons we covered in how much life insurance you actually need: the purpose is protection, and term cover provides the most of it per rupee. Record the board resolution that approves the policy and explains its business purpose. Review the sum assured every two or three years as the business grows. And make sure the key person's personal cover is separate and adequate, so the family is not relying on the business policy for its own protection.

Where this leaves you

A business built around one person carries a risk that does not appear on its balance sheet. Key person cover puts a number on that risk and transfers it. Paired with a funded buy-sell agreement, it ensures that the loss of a founder is a painful event for the business rather than a fatal one.

Frequently asked questions

What is key person insurance?
It is a life insurance policy taken by a business on the life of an individual whose death would cause it financial loss. The business pays the premium and receives the payout.
Who can be covered under keyman insurance?
Promoters, directors, partners or employees whose skills, relationships or guarantees are critical to the business. The cover must be justified by the person's economic importance to the company.
Is keyman insurance premium tax deductible?
Premiums paid by the business for a genuine keyman policy have generally been treated as a deductible business expense. The payout is taxable as business income. Confirm the current provisions under the Income-tax Act, 2025 with your CA.
How much key person cover should a business buy?
It is usually based on the person's contribution to profit, the cost of replacing them, and the debts they guarantee. Insurers cap cover based on the company's financials.
What is a buy-sell agreement?
It is an agreement between business partners that the survivors will buy a deceased partner's shares at an agreed price. Life insurance on each partner usually funds the purchase.
