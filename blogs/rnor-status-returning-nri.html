Moving Back to India: The RNOR Window and How Not to Waste It

When a long-term NRI moves back to India, they do not become a full Indian taxpayer overnight. There is a transition period, usually two to three years, in which most foreign income stays outside the Indian tax net. This is RNOR status, short for resident but not ordinarily resident, and it is one of the most valuable and least planned-for windows in Indian tax.

By SELEQT Editorial

Most returnees discover it after the fact, when the window is half gone. The families who benefit most are the ones who plan the move with it in mind.

Three categories, not two

Indian tax law recognises three residential statuses. A non-resident is taxed only on income earned or received in India. A resident and ordinarily resident, or ROR, is taxed on worldwide income. Between them sits the resident but not ordinarily resident, who is taxed on Indian income but generally not on foreign income, unless it is received in India or comes from a business controlled from India.

The Income-tax Act, 2025, which took effect from April 2026, carries forward the same framework. Some section numbers and terms have changed, with "tax year" replacing the old "previous year", but the substance of the test is the same.

Who qualifies

First, you must be resident in the year, typically because you spend 182 days or more in India. You are then treated as RNOR, rather than ROR, if either of two conditions applies. You were a non-resident in nine of the ten preceding years, or you spent 729 days or fewer in India across the seven preceding years.

For someone who has lived abroad for a decade or more, both conditions are usually met in the year of return and often for one or two years after. The exact length of the window depends on your travel history, which is why counting days carefully from passport stamps and travel records is the first step of any return plan.

There is also a separate route. An Indian citizen whose Indian income exceeds ₹15 lakh and who spends 120 days or more in India, with 365 days across the previous four years, can be treated as resident, and in that case is automatically RNOR. This mostly affects frequent visitors rather than those returning permanently.

What the window actually protects

During RNOR years, income that arises outside India and is received outside India is generally not taxed in India. That covers salary for work done abroad before the move, interest on foreign bank deposits, dividends from foreign shares, rent from property abroad, and, importantly, capital gains on selling foreign assets.

That last point is the most valuable. An NRI holding appreciated foreign shares, a foreign property, or vested RSUs who sells them during the RNOR window may owe no Indian tax on the gain. The same sale made a year or two later, as an ROR, would be fully taxable in India, subject to treaty relief for any foreign tax paid.

The RNOR window does not override the tax laws of the country you are leaving. A US citizen or green card holder remains taxable in the US on worldwide income. Other countries may tax gains for some period after departure. The plan has to work on both sides.

The account changes you must make

Your bank accounts need to change too, and here the relevant law is FEMA rather than the Income-tax Act. Under FEMA, you become resident as soon as you return with the intention of staying, regardless of the RNOR position for tax.

NRE and NRO accounts must be redesignated as resident accounts, and the bank should be told promptly. FCNR deposits can usually be held until maturity. A Resident Foreign Currency (RFC) account allows returnees to keep foreign currency in India without converting it, which is useful for those who expect to spend or invest abroad later. Foreign bank accounts and investments acquired while you were an NRI can generally be retained.

Common ways the window gets wasted

Selling at the wrong time. Selling foreign assets just before returning, while still an NRI, is often fine. Selling them after becoming ROR is often expensive. The worst outcome is simply not getting around to it and drifting into ROR status with large unrealised foreign gains.

Bringing the money in too early. Foreign income is protected only if it is received outside India. Proceeds of a sale made abroad and then transferred to India are capital, which is generally fine. But income that is first received in an Indian account can fall into the tax net.

Ignoring reporting. Once you become ROR, every foreign asset must be disclosed in the foreign assets schedule of your return each year, as we explained in the tax on your US stocks and foreign ETFs. The RNOR years are the time to simplify the foreign portfolio so that this reporting is manageable.

Not planning the timing of the move itself. Arriving in April or in October can change how many days fall in which tax year. For families with significant foreign assets, the date of the flight is a tax decision.

A sensible sequence

Count your days and confirm how many RNOR years you are likely to have. List every foreign asset, its cost and its current value. Decide which assets you will hold for the long term and which you will realise during the window. Coordinate with a tax adviser in the country you are leaving. Redesignate your Indian accounts and open an RFC account if needed. Then build the Indian portfolio deliberately, rather than leaving the proceeds in a savings account while you settle in.

Where this leaves you

The RNOR window is temporary, and it closes whether or not you use it. For a returning family with meaningful foreign assets, a few months of planning before the move can save more tax than years of optimisation afterwards. Plan the return as carefully as you planned the move abroad.

Frequently asked questions

What is RNOR status?
Resident but not ordinarily resident is a transitional tax status for people who become Indian residents after a long period abroad. RNORs are taxed on Indian income, but generally not on foreign income received outside India.
How long does RNOR status last?
Typically two to three tax years after returning, depending on your travel history. It applies while you were a non-resident in nine of the previous ten years, or spent 729 days or fewer in India over the previous seven years.
Is foreign income taxable for an RNOR?
Generally not, if it arises and is received outside India and does not come from a business controlled from India. Foreign income received in India is taxable.
What happens to my NRE account when I return to India?
It must be redesignated as a resident account once you return for good. FCNR deposits can usually run to maturity, and an RFC account can hold foreign currency in India.
Should I sell my foreign investments before or after returning?
It depends on the tax rules in both countries. In many cases, selling during the RNOR window avoids Indian tax on the gain. Get advice on both sides before deciding.
