The Family Investment Policy Statement: The Document That Decides Before the Market Does

Most family portfolios are built one decision at a time. A fund here because a relative recommended it, a property there because the opportunity felt right, a PMS because a banker called. Each choice made sense in the moment. Together they rarely add up to a plan. An investment policy statement is the document that replaces that drift with a set of rules the family agrees to while everyone is calm.

By SELEQT Editorial

Endowments, pension funds and institutional family offices have used policy statements for decades. They are not complicated. What makes them valuable is that they are written before they are needed.

What it is, in one sentence

An investment policy statement, or IPS, sets out what the family's money is for, how much risk it can carry, how it will be allocated, who decides what, and how the results will be judged. It is usually ten to fifteen pages. It is not a forecast, a product list or a legal contract. It is a written agreement with your future self, and with the rest of the family, about how decisions will be made.

Why families need it more than institutions do

An endowment has a board, a committee and staff whose job is to follow process. A family has a patriarch or matriarch, two or three siblings with different views, a spouse who was never consulted, and a next generation that may inherit the outcome without inheriting the reasoning.

That is precisely the setting in which unwritten rules fail. When markets fall sharply, the person who argues loudest tends to win. When one sibling wants liquidity and another wants growth, the absence of an agreed framework turns an investment question into a family argument. An IPS does not eliminate disagreement. It moves it to a quiet room, months before the crisis, where it can be settled on its merits.

What goes into a good one

The first section is purpose. What is this pool of money for? Funding the family's lifestyle, preserving capital across generations, supporting a business, philanthropy, or some combination. Each purpose implies a different time horizon, and a single portfolio often has to serve several.

The second is the return objective, stated in real terms. "Inflation plus 4% after tax over rolling seven-year periods" is a usable objective. "As high as possible" is not. We explained why the real figure is the one that matters in your real return after tax and inflation.

The third is risk tolerance, described as behaviour rather than as a label. The question is not whether the family is "moderate". It is how large a fall, in rupees, the family could watch without changing course. A portfolio of ₹50 crore that falls 25% is down ₹12.5 crore. If that number would force a sale, the allocation is wrong, however reasonable it looked on paper.

The fourth is liquidity. How much cash will the family draw each year, what large outflows are expected in the next five years, and how much must always be available at short notice.

The fifth is strategic asset allocation with ranges. For example, listed equity at 50% with a permitted range of 40% to 60%, fixed income at 30%, alternatives at 15%, and cash at 5%. The ranges are as important as the targets, because they tell everyone when rebalancing is required and when it is not.

The sections most families skip

Rebalancing rules turn a target into a discipline. If equity rises above its upper limit, part of the gain is trimmed. If it falls below its lower limit, it is topped up. Written in advance, this forces the family to buy when prices are depressed and sell when they are elevated, which is the opposite of instinct.

Permitted and prohibited investments prevent the portfolio from being hijacked by enthusiasm. A family might rule out unlisted loans to acquaintances, cap any single private investment at 5% of assets, or exclude leveraged products altogether.

Decision rights answer who can approve what. Routine rebalancing may sit with the adviser. A new manager may need the family's investment committee. Anything above a set size may need unanimous agreement.

Concentration limits matter particularly for business families, whose largest asset is often the operating company. The IPS should say explicitly whether the business is inside or outside the investable portfolio, and how much additional exposure to the same sector the family will tolerate.

How it is used in practice

The IPS is reviewed formally once a year and whenever something material changes: a liquidity event, a death, a marriage, a move abroad, or a change in the family business. Every quarterly review is measured against it. Did the portfolio stay within its ranges? Did each manager meet the benchmark the IPS specified? Did withdrawals stay within the planned rate?

Its most important use, however, is during bad markets. When a portfolio is down sharply and someone proposes moving everything to deposits, the IPS is what allows the family to ask a better question: has anything changed about our purpose, horizon or liquidity needs? If the answer is no, the plan stands.

When it is overkill

A young professional with a few mutual funds does not need a formal IPS. A simple written note of goals and allocation is enough. The document earns its keep once wealth is shared across several family members, spread across several advisers or structures, or large enough that a single bad decision would change the family's future. At that point the cost of writing one is trivial compared with the cost of not having one.

Where this leaves you

The best investment decisions are made before they are needed. An investment policy statement turns the family's intentions into rules, and rules into a portfolio that can survive both markets and family meetings. It is the least glamorous document a family will own, and often the one that does the most work.

Frequently asked questions

What is an investment policy statement?
It is a written document that sets out a family's investment objectives, risk tolerance, liquidity needs, asset allocation, decision rights and review process. It guides every investment decision. Institutions have used them for decades.
Is an IPS a legal document?
Usually not. It is a governance document that the family and its advisers agree to follow. Some families reference it in a trust deed or family constitution to give it more weight.
Who should write the investment policy statement?
It is normally drafted by the family's adviser or family office, through detailed conversations with the decision-makers. The family should own the final version and understand every section of it.
How often should it be reviewed?
Formally once a year, and immediately after any major event such as a business sale, a death, a marriage or a change of residence. Market movements alone are not a reason to rewrite it.
Does a family with one adviser still need an IPS?
Yes, if the wealth is significant. The IPS protects the family's intentions regardless of which adviser is in place, and makes it easy to judge whether that adviser is doing the job.
