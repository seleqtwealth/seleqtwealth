After You Sell the Business: The First Twelve Months of a Liquidity Event

For most founders, the sale of a business is the largest financial event of their lives. Years of illiquid, concentrated, deeply understood wealth become, in a single transfer, a large sum of cash. A liquidity event changes more than a bank balance. It changes the founder's job from building one asset to managing many, and the skills that made the first fortune are not always the ones that protect it.

By SELEQT Editorial

The decisions made in the year before and the year after a sale tend to shape the family's wealth for a generation. Most of the costly mistakes are not about investment returns. They are about structure, tax and timing.

Before signing: structure decides tax

The way a sale is structured often matters more to the final number than the headline valuation. A sale of shares is taxed differently from a sale of the business's assets. Shares held personally are taxed differently from shares held through a holding company. Consideration paid upfront is treated differently from an earn-out, deferred payment or escrow that depends on future performance.

For unlisted shares held for more than 24 months, long-term capital gains are currently taxed at 12.5% without indexation. The new Income-tax Act carries forward the relief for reinvesting gains from assets other than a house into a residential house, with the deduction capped at ₹10 crore. Gains on listed shares held for more than a year are taxed at 12.5% above ₹1.25 lakh. We covered these rates in the new capital gains rules.

If family members are shareholders, if some are NRIs, or if the buyer is overseas, the tax position of each holder and the currency of payment should be settled well before the term sheet is signed. Once the deal is done, the options narrow sharply.

The first ninety days: do less than you think

The most common mistake after a sale is acting too quickly. Founders are used to making decisions, and a large cash balance feels like a problem to be solved. Advisers, bankers and acquaintances will arrive with opportunities, many of them genuine, some of them not.

The sensible response is to do very little at first. Pay or reserve the tax. Place the proceeds in high-quality, liquid instruments such as liquid funds, arbitrage funds, treasury bills or short-term government securities, spread across institutions. Then take time to think. A few months of earning a modest, safe return costs very little. A rushed commitment to an illiquid investment can cost a great deal.

Separate the money by purpose

Once the dust has settled, it helps to divide the proceeds into distinct pools, each with its own job and time horizon.

The first is security: enough to fund the family's lifestyle for many years, regardless of what markets do, held conservatively, as we described in building the conservative part of your portfolio. The second is long-term growth: a diversified portfolio of listed equity, high-quality debt and selected alternatives, built to compound across decades. The third is opportunity: a defined, limited amount for the founder to invest in new ventures, angel deals or a second business, with the understanding that some of it may be lost.

The third pool matters more than it seems. Most founders will want to invest in businesses again. Giving that instinct a defined budget protects the rest of the family's wealth from it.

Concentration does not end with the sale

Founders who sell often reinvest heavily in what they know: the same sector, the same network, sometimes the acquiring company's shares. After a lifetime of building wealth through one concentrated bet, a second concentrated bet feels natural. But the conditions that made the first one work, including control, information and daily involvement, are rarely present in the second.

If part of the consideration is paid in the buyer's shares, decide in advance how and when those shares will be diversified, subject to any lock-in. A plan made before the lock-in expires is far easier to follow than a decision made in the moment.

Put structure around the wealth

A liquidity event is the natural moment to put governance in place. An investment policy statement sets out how the money will be invested and who decides. Wills should be rewritten, since the estate is now very different. For larger sums, a private trust can hold the wealth for the next generation. Where several family members are involved, a family constitution can settle expectations before they turn into disagreements.

For families considering moving part of their wealth abroad, the Liberalised Remittance Scheme allows each resident individual to remit up to USD 250,000 a year. A family of four can therefore move a meaningful amount over several years, but it requires planning, not a single transfer.

The part nobody prepares for

Many founders find the year after a sale harder than they expected, and not for financial reasons. The business gave structure to their days, a team, and a sense of identity. Its absence can create pressure to do something, anything, quickly.

The families who handle this well tend to treat the first year as a transition rather than a starting gun. They take advice from people who are not selling them anything, involve their spouse and children in the conversation early, and give themselves permission to move slowly.

Where this leaves you

Selling a business turns a builder into a steward. The capital that took decades to create can be diminished in a few years by tax inefficiency, haste or a second concentrated bet. Plan the structure before the sale, keep the first months quiet, and build the governance while the decision is still fresh.

Frequently asked questions

What should I do with money after selling my business?
First, settle or reserve the tax and park the proceeds in safe, liquid instruments across several institutions. Then take time to plan before making long-term or illiquid commitments.
How is the sale of unlisted shares taxed in India?
Unlisted shares held for more than 24 months are taxed at 12.5% as long-term capital gains, without indexation. Shorter holdings are taxed at slab rates. Reinvesting in a residential house may reduce the tax, subject to a ₹10 crore cap.
Should I sell shares or assets when selling my business?
It depends on the tax position of the sellers and the buyer's preferences. Share sales are usually simpler for sellers, while asset sales can suit buyers. The structure should be decided with tax advice before negotiations conclude.
How should a founder diversify after a sale?
Divide the proceeds into pools for security, long-term growth and new opportunities. Limit further concentration in a single sector or company, and plan the sale of any shares received from the buyer.
When should succession planning start after a liquidity event?
Immediately. A sale changes the size and nature of the estate, so wills, trusts and family governance should be reviewed within the first year.
